# F-508071ce1092

## Purpose

F-508071ce1092 combines two arrays of unsigned 32-bit words into a caller-supplied destination and publishes a 16-bit destination length.

**Inferred intent:** multiplication of nonnegative, multiple-precision integers represented as least-significant-word-first arrays. The implementation processes the second operand two words at a time, followed by a one-word tail when necessary.

**Established limitation:** ordinary multiplication is not an unconditional contract. An unresolved dependency is invoked before processing, and its effects are not supplied. The arithmetic subsequently reads destination words before overwriting them. Correct multiplication therefore depends on destination initialization, storage layout, capacity, and noninterference conditions described below.

There is no returned numeric value, allocation result, or error status.

## Interface

The function receives three descriptor addresses:

| Argument | Observed descriptor fields | Use |
|---|---|---|
| First operand | Unsigned 16-bit count at byte offset 0; 32-bit data-address field at byte offset 4 | Supplies the first word array |
| Second operand | Unsigned 16-bit count at byte offset 0; 32-bit data-address field at byte offset 4 | Supplies the second word array |
| Destination | 16-bit length field at byte offset 0; 32-bit data-address field at byte offset 4 | Supplies writable storage and receives the final length |

The source views the destination length field as signed 16-bit storage. Consumers must distinguish its stored bit pattern from a nonnegative mathematical length.

The two bytes between the count and data-address fields have no directly observed use. A larger descriptor layout is not established.

Let:

- \(m\) be the initially read first-operand count;
- \(n\) be the initially read second-operand count;
- \(B=2^{32}\);
- \(a_i\) and \(b_j\) denote operand words when their reads occur.

Both counts lie in \(0\) through \(65{,}535\). Operand words are unsigned 32-bit quantities. Individual word products use unsigned 64-bit arithmetic.

### Preconditions

For defined, successful execution:

- Every accessed descriptor field must be readable.
- The destination length field must be writable.
- Every data address actually used must designate accessible storage of sufficient size and suitable alignment.
- The initial dependency must return normally.
- Data and descriptor aliasing must not invalidate subsequent reads or writes.

For the intended multiplication interpretation, stronger conditions are necessary:

- Operand data must remain stable throughout processing.
- Destination writes must not corrupt operand data or descriptor fields needed later.
- The destination must be initialized appropriately before accumulation.
- A 64-bit destination store must place its low 32 bits at the lower word address and its high 32 bits at the next address.
- The destination must accommodate all accesses, including the zero-count exceptions below.

No null checks, capacity checks, overlap checks, or explicit error branches are visible.

## Behavior

### Entry and dependency invocation

The function first reads the first operand’s count and then the second operand’s count. These values are retained for subsequent loop bounds and the final length calculation.

It next reads the destination data-address field and invokes an unresolved dependency with:

1. That address.
2. A byte quantity equal to \(4(m+n)\).

The sum is computed before the later 16-bit length reduction. Its maximum is \(131{,}070\), so the maximum byte quantity is \(524{,}280\); neither exceeds unsigned 32-bit range.

The supplied evidence does not establish whether the dependency clears storage, allocates storage, changes descriptors, or has other effects. In particular, it must not be assumed to preserve globals, descriptor fields, operand contents, or ownership.

After this call, the function reads the second operand’s data-address field. Consequently, operand counts come from before the call, whereas this data address comes from after it.

### Paired processing of the second operand

The function processes second-operand words in pairs, beginning at word offsets \(0,2,4,\ldots\). There are exactly \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor\) paired passes.

Each paired pass:

- Reads both words of its second-operand pair.
- Reads the destination data-address field again.
- Reads the destination word at the pair’s starting offset.
- Reads the first operand’s data-address field again.
- Traverses exactly \(m\) first-operand words.
- Finishes with one 64-bit destination store.

The second-operand traversal address advances continuously between pairs. It is not reloaded from its descriptor for each pair.

The first operand and destination addresses, by contrast, are reloaded for each paired pass. Descriptor mutations can therefore affect later passes even though the original counts remain fixed.

### Arithmetic of a paired pass

The following mathematical description applies when destination words have the low-word-first layout required by the inferred multiplication representation.

For a pair starting at offset \(j\), write its two words as \(p=b_j\) and \(q=b_{j+1}\). The pass accumulates the contribution of these two words into destination positions beginning at \(j\).

A carry quantity starts at zero, and the predecessor of the first first-operand word is treated as zero. For each first-operand position \(i\), the arithmetic combines:

\[
a_i p + a_{i-1}q + d_{j+i} + c_i,
\]

where \(a_{-1}=0\), \(d_{j+i}\) is the destination word retained for this position, and \(c_i\) is the incoming carry.

The destination position receives the low 32 bits of this sum. The quotient on division by \(B\) becomes the next carry. This carry can require more than 32 bits; the implementation explicitly tracks the necessary carry components rather than treating it as one ordinary word.

The destination read/write ordering matters:

- The current first-operand word is read.
- The next destination word is read and retained.
- The current destination word is written.
- Traversal advances by one word.

Thus, the destination word used by the next iteration was read before the current write. This is observable under overlap or external mutation and is not interchangeable with rereading it later.

After the \(m\) iterations, the pass forms a final unsigned 64-bit quantity from:

- The second word of the pair multiplied by the last first-operand word, or zero if \(m=0\).
- The retained destination word at offset \(j+m\).
- The remaining carry.

The final quantity is reduced modulo \(2^{64}\) and stored with one 64-bit write beginning at destination word offset \(j+m\). Its high 32 bits become the carry state used by the final length decision.

This store replaces two destination words. It does **not** first read and add the existing destination word at offset \(j+m+1\). Therefore, with arbitrary preexisting destination contents, a paired pass is not simply an unrestricted full-width addition into the entire existing destination integer.

### Odd-word tail

If \(n\) is odd, one second-operand word remains after the paired passes. The function reads that word.

If it is zero:

- No destination address is reloaded for the tail.
- No tail arithmetic or tail destination write occurs.
- The carry state from the preceding paired pass remains in effect.
- If no paired pass occurred, that state is still zero.

If the remaining word is nonzero:

- The carry state is reset to zero.
- The destination data-address field is read again.
- The first operand’s data-address field is read again.
- Exactly \(m\) first-operand words are processed.

Each position combines the first-operand word times the remaining second-operand word, the corresponding destination word, and the incoming carry. The low 32 bits are written to that destination position; the high 32 bits become the next carry.

This sum fits in unsigned 64 bits: its maximum is \(B^2-1\).

After the traversal, the final carry is written as one 32-bit word immediately after the processed region. That final write still occurs when \(m=0\), provided the remaining second-operand word was nonzero.

The supplied expression does not establish a separate guaranteed ordering between the operand-word read and destination-word read within a tail iteration. Both supply the value written by that iteration.

### Final length publication

After all arithmetic, the function computes:

\[
L=(m+n)\bmod 65{,}536.
\]

The visible mask produces this result because the unreduced sum cannot exceed \(131{,}070\).

The stored 16-bit length pattern is:

- \(L-1\), if \(L\ne0\) and the final carry state is zero;
- \(L\), otherwise.

This is a single-step adjustment, not general leading-zero normalization. The function does not scan the destination for additional zero high words.

The final carry state is:

- The high half of the final paired-pass store, when paired processing is last.
- The tail carry, when a nonzero odd tail is processed.
- The prior paired-pass carry when an odd tail word is zero.
- Zero when neither path changes the initial carry.

The destination’s prior length is never consulted.

### Zero-count and boundary behavior

Zero counts do not universally suppress data accesses.

- If \(n=0\), there are no operand-data reads or arithmetic destination writes after the dependency call.
- If \(m=0\) and a pair exists, every paired pass still reads one destination word and performs a 64-bit destination write.
- If \(m=0\) and the odd tail word is nonzero, the tail still writes a zero carry word.
- If \(m=n=0\), the dependency is called with a byte quantity of zero, and the destination length is ultimately set to zero.

For \(m=0\) and \(n>0\), arithmetic writes can extend one word beyond the \(m+n\) words suggested by the dependency’s byte quantity. Allocation of exactly that requested size is therefore not a sufficient general capacity guarantee.

Loop-index updates clear bit 16, but each traversal reaches its stopping bound before that clearing changes the required sequence. The count limits therefore do not cause an additional loop traversal or a wraparound loop.

## Side effects

Directly established effects are:

- One invocation of the unresolved dependency.
- Reads of descriptor fields, operand words, and selected destination words.
- In-place destination writes during paired and tail processing.
- A final write to the destination’s 16-bit length field.

The function itself contains no explicit allocation, deallocation, ownership transfer, or direct global-state access. These absences do not constrain the unresolved dependency.

No transactional behavior is visible. If an access or dependency invocation fails, no rollback or recoverable error result is established.

Aliasing has no separately defined safe mode. Because pointers are reloaded at different times, writes overlapping descriptors can change later addresses. Writes overlapping operands can change later arithmetic inputs. The ordinary multiplication interpretation excludes such interference; it does not silently specify a temporary copy.

## Acceptance examples

These examples are conditional specifications, not executed tests.

Unless stated otherwise, assume F-UNKNOWN-01 leaves the relevant descriptors and operand data unchanged, zeroes sufficient destination storage, and the target uses the required low-word-first layout for 64-bit stores.

1. **One-word product without a high word**

   With \(m=n=1\), first word \(3\), and second word \(5\), the tail writes destination words \(15\) and \(0\). The final length is \(1\).

2. **One-word product with a nonzero high word**

   With \(m=n=1\) and both words equal to \(B-1\), the destination words are \(1\) and \(B-2\). The final length is \(2\).

3. **A complete two-word pair**

   With first operand \([1]\) and second operand \([2,3]\), the destination words are \([2,3,0]\). The final length is \(2\).

4. **Only one leading zero is removed**

   With \(m=3\), \(n=0\), the arithmetic performs no destination-data writes after the dependency. The final length is \(2\), regardless of whether the destination represents zero.

5. **An all-zero two-word second operand is not fully normalized**

   With \(m=1\), first operand \([7]\), and second operand \([0,0]\), the destination words are zero, but the final length is \(2\), not zero.

6. **A zero odd tail preserves an earlier carry**

   With first operand \([B-1]\) and second operand \([B-1,B-1,0]\), the destination words are \([1,B-1,B-2,0]\). The zero tail does not reset the paired-pass carry, so the published length is \(4\), despite the zero highest destination word.

7. **Zero first count still permits an outlying write**

   With \(m=0\), \(n=1\), and second operand \([9]\), the dependency receives a byte quantity of \(4\). The arithmetic subsequently writes zero at destination word offset \(1\), requiring storage beyond those four bytes. The published length is zero.

8. **Length reduction can discard a valid large size**

   With \(m=65{,}535\) and \(n=1\), the dependency receives \(262{,}144\) bytes. If execution completes, the final length pattern is zero because \(m+n=65{,}536\); the nonzero-length decrement condition cannot change that result.
