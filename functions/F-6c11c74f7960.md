## Behavior

### Address derivation

The third argument first undergoes a signed right shift by 11 bit positions.

The resulting value contributes to the address in two ways:

1. Its own 32-bit value contributes one addend.
2. Its 32-bit unsigned interpretation is logically shifted right by two positions to obtain the other addend.

The addends are combined using 32-bit unsigned arithmetic. Consequently, the addition is reduced modulo \(2^{32}\). The resulting value is then interpreted as the destination address.

The unsigned conversion before the second shift is significant. For a negative intermediate value, that second shift is a logical shift of its unsigned bit pattern, not a signed division by four.

The supplied representation treats the third argument as signed. For nonnegative arguments, its first right shift has an unambiguous numerical interpretation: division by 2048 with the fractional part discarded. For negative arguments, arithmetic right shift is a plausible interpretation, but the actual machine shift semantics are not independently established by the supplied material. Negative-input expectations below are therefore conditional on arithmetic right shift.

Under that condition, the address calculation can be described mathematically as:

\[
\operatorname{unsigned32}\left(
\left\lfloor\frac{\text{third argument}}{2048}\right\rfloor
+
\left\lfloor
\frac{
\operatorname{unsigned32}\left(
\left\lfloor\frac{\text{third argument}}{2048}\right\rfloor
\right)
}{4}
\right\rfloor
\right).
\]

Here, `unsigned32` means reduction to a value from zero through \(2^{32}-1\). This expression describes an address value, not an array-element number.

The calculation loses the lowest 11 bits of a nonnegative input. Thus, groups of 2048 consecutive nonnegative inputs share a destination address. Increasing the shifted value by one usually increases the address by one, but crossing a multiple of four increases it by two. This behavior does not imply a known record or pixel layout.
