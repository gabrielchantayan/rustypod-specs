# F-dd16d490244f

## Purpose

F-dd16d490244f specifies a repeating sequence of indirect calls, byte accesses, 32-bit memory transfers, and state replacement. Results obtained during one cycle determine later addresses, call arguments, stored values, and inputs to the next cycle.

No normal return, termination condition, status result, or explicit recovery path appears in the supplied representation. If all calls return and all required operations succeed, execution continues indefinitely.

The application-level purpose is unknown. The evidence does not identify the addressed memory as objects, buffers, device registers, or dispatch tables.

This contract describes static data dependencies and source-level ordering. It does not guarantee that every represented store becomes separately observable memory traffic. Volatile semantics, synchronization, and compiler treatment are not established.

### Arithmetic and terminology

A **scaled result** means a 32-bit result shifted left by two. A **selector update** comprises an unsigned eight-bit read followed by an eight-bit store at the numeric address equal to the selector plus 17.

Consequently:

- Selector-derived byte destinations range from 17 through 272.
- A word destination formed from a captured selector plus 20 ranges from 20 through 275.
- These are absolute numeric address constructions; no containing object is established.

Unsigned masks and right shifts have explicit 32-bit semantics. Signed multiplication and signed left shifts do not establish portable behavior for overflowing or otherwise invalid operands. Statements about wrapping, discarded high bits, or low-bit dependence below are conditional on a 32-bit machine interpretation.

For byte extraction, **extract then truncate** means:

\[
\operatorname{truncate}_{8}\!\left((W \mathbin{\&} \text{0x3fffffff}) \gg 20\right).
\]

The mask and shift act on the full 32-bit word. Conversion to eight bits occurs afterward.

## Behavior

### Ordering conventions

The following phases occur in order on each completed cycle. Counts of calls exclude the argument-bearing call immediately preceding the counted group.

A group of four calls means four consecutive indirect calls with no explicitly displayed arguments; where a result is specified, it comes from the fourth call. Other implicit inputs remain unknown.

Memory operations are distinct operations in the supplied representation. Their ordering here is not a guarantee of volatile access, atomicity, or separate external observability.

### 1. Opening update and first word transfer

The opening selector is read at the current selector base plus 1. The current entry byte is stored at that selector plus 17.

The opening dependency then receives the current first argument. Four calls follow. Their final result, scaled by four, becomes the **opening word**.

The opening word is stored at:

\[
4096 \times \text{current fourth argument} + 20.
\]

A four-argument call follows, receiving, in order:

1. The current fourth argument multiplied by 1024.
2. That same product.
3. The additional incoming 32-bit value shifted left by 12.
4. The opening word.

Four calls produce another index. The opening word is stored at four times that index plus 20. A call then receives four times that index.

The two stores remain distinct source-level operations even if their destinations coincide.

### 2. Two scaled-word transactions

Each transaction has this structure:

- Four calls produce a first index.
- Four times that index is stored as a word at 16 times the index plus 20.
- A three-argument call receives that scaled value in every argument position.
- Four calls produce a second index.
- The same word is stored at four times the second index plus 20.
- A call receives four times the second index.

During the first transaction, a retained zero byte is established for subsequent direct clears.

During the second transaction, the first index also generates a **retained byte base**, equal to that index multiplied by \(2^{30}\). Under wrapping 32-bit arithmetic, only the index’s low two bits affect this base.

The second index from the second transaction is retained for a later word value.

### 3. Direct clears, indexed read, and retained payload

Four calls produce an unsigned 32-bit result, called the **read-address result**.

Two direct byte stores follow: zero at the retained byte base plus 17, then zero at that base plus 1. These are not selector updates.

A call receives the read-address result shifted left by two. The next call produces a 32-bit value, which is passed to a following one-argument call.

Four calls produce another result. The same pair of direct zero-byte stores follows, again at offset 17 before offset 1. A call receives the latest result shifted left by two.

Four calls then produce a new index. A word is stored at 16 times that index plus 20. Its value is the second index retained from the preceding phase shifted left by 31. Under a wrapping interpretation, only that earlier index’s low bit determines whether the stored word is zero or `0x80000000`.

Next, a 32-bit unsigned word is read at:

\[
20 + ((\text{read-address result} \mathbin{\&} \text{0x3fffffff}) \gg 20).
\]

The starting address ranges from 20 through 1043 and need not be word-aligned.

A call receives four times the latest index. That scaled index is retained for a later call. The next call produces a separate **retained payload**.

A following call receives the fetched word shifted right by 22 and then left by 28. These are successive 32-bit unsigned shifts; the operation does not preserve the complete ten-bit result of the right shift.

### 4. Selector updates and a captured-selector word destination

Four calls produce an index. A zero word is stored at four times that index plus 20, followed by a call receiving four times the index.

Four calls produce an unsigned **selector payload**. A fresh byte read at the retained byte base plus 1 supplies a selector. Its destination receives the low eight bits of the selector payload shifted left by two.

A call receives that same 32-bit shifted payload. The next call produces a value passed to a two-argument call, whose second argument is the selector payload shifted left by 30.

Four calls produce another index. The selector at the retained byte base plus 1 is read again and captured. Its byte destination receives:

\[
\operatorname{truncate}_{8}\!\left(
(\text{selector payload} \mathbin{\&} \text{0x3fffffff}) \gg 20
\right).
\]

The extraction before truncation ranges from 0 through 1023.

A call receives the latest index shifted left by two. Four calls follow. A word containing the retained payload shifted left by two is then stored at the **captured selector plus 20**.

This word destination does not use a fresh selector read. Changes made to the selector location by intervening dependencies do not redirect this particular store.

### 5. Global snapshot and first split result

A 32-bit unsigned global value is read once and captured. A call receives the scaled index retained during phase 3.

Four calls produce a represented 64-bit result. Its low 32 bits, shifted left by two, become the **first split scale**.

A fresh selector read at the retained byte base plus 1 determines a byte destination. That destination receives the eight-bit truncation of the global snapshot shifted right by 22. The shift precedes truncation.

The high 32 bits of the 64-bit result are passed to the next call. Another call produces a value passed to a two-argument call, whose second argument is the first split scale shifted left by 28.

Four calls produce a result. A fresh selector update at the retained byte base plus 1 stores zero, after which a call receives that result shifted left by two.

Four calls produce another result. A two-argument call receives that result shifted left by two in both positions.

Later global changes do not alter the already captured snapshot. No assumption is made that dependencies preserve the global.

### 6. Middle transfer and replacement selector base

Four calls produce a result whose scaled value becomes the **middle word**. It is stored at:

\[
4096 \times \text{first split scale} + 20.
\]

A call receives the first split scale shifted left by 14.

Four calls produce an index. The middle word is stored at four times that index plus 20, followed by a call receiving four times the index.

Four calls produce a second represented 64-bit result. Its low half, shifted left by two, becomes the **second split scale**.

Before the high half is passed onward, a selector is read at:

\[
16384 \times \text{first split scale} + 1.
\]

The selector-derived byte destination receives:

\[
\operatorname{truncate}_{8}\!\left(
(\text{middle word} \mathbin{\&} \text{0x3fffffff}) \gg 20
\right).
\]

In particular, the middle word is **not** truncated to a byte before masking and shifting.

The second split result’s high half is passed to a call. Another call produces a value passed to the following one-argument call.

The next call produces a third represented 64-bit result. Its low and high halves are passed, in that order, to a two-argument call. This third result does not replace the second split scale.

Two calls produce an index. A selector read at the second split scale multiplied by \(2^{28}\), plus 1, determines a byte destination receiving zero. A call receives the index shifted left by two.

Four calls produce a **late transfer result**. The selector-base state is then replaced by four times the second split scale. A fresh selector update at this replacement base plus 1 stores zero.

### 7. Final transfer and next-cycle state

A call receives the second split scale shifted left by 10. Another call produces a value passed to a two-argument call. Its second argument is the **retained payload from phase 3**, shifted left by 28—not the late transfer result.

Four calls produce an index. A fresh selector update at the replacement base plus 1 stores zero. A call receives the index shifted left by two, followed by four calls.

The **final word** is the late transfer result shifted left by 14. It is stored at:

\[
4096 \times (4 \times \text{late transfer result}) + 20.
\]

A call receives four times the late transfer result, shifted left by 14. Another call follows. A two-argument call then receives:

1. The final word shifted left by 28.
2. A secondary machine result whose producer is not fully identified by the displayed return notation.

Four calls produce an index. The final word is stored again at four times that index plus 20. A call receives four times the index.

Four calls produce a result. Its scaled value becomes both the next fourth argument and the additional incoming 32-bit state used early in the next cycle.

A fresh selector read at the replacement base plus 1 determines a destination receiving:

\[
\operatorname{truncate}_{8}\!\left(
(\text{final word} \mathbin{\&} \text{0x3fffffff}) \gg 20
\right).
\]

Again, extraction uses the full word before byte truncation.

The remaining calls, in order:

- Receive the late transfer result shifted left by 26.
- Produce a value.
- Receive that value and the next fourth argument shifted left by 28.
- Produce a represented 64-bit result.
- Receive its low and high halves separately, in that order.
- Make one further call.
- Produce the result whose scaled value becomes the next first argument.

The next entry byte is set to zero between the last 64-bit result and the call consuming its halves. The opening control-location state is replaced before the final result-producing call. Execution repeats using the replacement selector base and updated state.

## Acceptance examples

These are conditional static deductions, not executed tests.

1. **Opening update.** A selector of 7 and entry byte of 165 prescribe a byte store of 165 at address 24 before the opening call.

2. **Opening word.** A fourth argument of 2 and opening result of 3 produce word value 12 at address 8212, assuming valid nonoverflowing arithmetic and memory.

3. **Direct clear ordering.** With retained byte base zero, each clear pair prescribes zero stores at address 17 and then address 1. This describes source-level operations, including when both bytes already contain zero; it does not promise separately observable writes.

4. **Extraction before truncation.** A middle word of `0x00100000` produces byte value 1. Truncating the word to eight bits first would produce zero and would not match the represented operation.

5. **Final extraction.** A late transfer result of 64 produces final word `0x00100000` under the stated arithmetic interpretation. Its later selector update therefore also stores byte value 1.

6. **Ten-bit extraction.** A word of `0x3ff00000` produces extracted value 1023 and stored byte 255.

7. **Captured selector.** A captured selector of 9 makes the later word destination address 29. An intervening change of the selector location to 12 does not redirect that store to address 32.

8. **Fresh selector.** If a dependency changes a selector between separate reads, a subsequent selector update uses the newly read value, subject to successful accesses.

9. **Indexed read boundary.** A read-address result of `0x3fffffff` gives starting address 1043 for the four-byte read. Whether that access succeeds is unresolved.

10. **Global snapshot.** A dependency changing the global after its read does not change the byte derived from the captured snapshot.

11. **Interrupted progression.** If a dependency never returns, subsequent operations and next-cycle entry are not required to occur.

12. **Loop-carried input.** A final result of 5 makes the next first argument 20 under 32-bit scaling. The next opening selector update uses byte value zero.
