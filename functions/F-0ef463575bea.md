## Behavior

### Selection rules

The result is determined by these mutually exclusive cases, in this order:

| Condition | Required result |
|---|---|
| The candidate is strictly less than the lower comparator | The lower comparator |
| The candidate is greater than or equal to the lower comparator, and strictly greater than the upper comparator | The upper comparator |
| The candidate is greater than or equal to the lower comparator, and less than or equal to the upper comparator | The candidate |

The first comparison is decisive when true. The function returns immediately in that case; the second comparison does not affect the result.

Only after the first comparison is false does the function compare the upper comparator with the candidate. That comparison is also strict. If the upper comparator is smaller, it becomes the selected result; otherwise, the candidate remains the selected result.

### Comparison and return ordering

The visible decision order is:

1. Compare the candidate with the lower comparator.
2. If the candidate is smaller, return the lower comparator.
3. Otherwise, compare the upper comparator with the candidate.
4. Return either the upper comparator or the unchanged candidate according to that second comparison.

This establishes the ordering of decisions within the function. It does not specify the order in which a caller evaluates argument expressions, how arguments are transported, or whether an implementation loads an argument before its comparison is reached.

### Ordered bounds

When the lower comparator is less than or equal to the upper comparator, the result lies within that inclusive interval:

- A candidate below the interval produces its lower endpoint.
- A candidate inside the interval produces itself.
- A candidate above the interval produces its upper endpoint.

Equality alone does not activate either strict comparison. A candidate equal to an endpoint of an ordered interval is therefore returned with that same numeric value.

When both comparators have the same value, every candidate produces that common value. Candidates below it select the lower comparator; candidates above it select the upper comparator; an equal candidate remains unchanged.

### Reversed bounds

Reversed bounds are not rejected, reordered, or normalized.

When the lower comparator is greater than the upper comparator:

- Every candidate strictly below the lower comparator produces the lower comparator.
- Every candidate greater than or equal to the lower comparator produces the upper comparator.

The second outcome follows because a candidate at least as large as the lower comparator must also be strictly greater than the upper comparator.

A candidate can therefore exceed the upper comparator and still return the lower comparator: the earlier comparison controls the result. An implementation that first limits the candidate against the upper comparator, swaps the bounds, or rejects reversed bounds would not satisfy this contract.
