# F-2d2490058d41

## Purpose

The supplied representation does not establish an application-level purpose. In particular, it does not justify identifying the operation as initialization, serialization, object construction, or device control. ### Width and address conventions

A byte access is 8 bits, a halfword access is 16 bits, and a word access is 32 bits.

Offsets are byte offsets. Expressions involving twice A are unsigned 32-bit arithmetic: both doubling and the subsequent addition wrap modulo \(2^{32}\). Address formation involving signed-represented values, and signed doubling later in the function, require target arithmetic semantics not established here.

## Behavior

### Repeated write prefix

The first phase repeatedly writes to four destinations:

| Destination | Access width | Stored value |
|---|---:|---|
| A plus `0x11` | 8 bits | Low byte of B |
| B plus `0x34` | 32 bits | V on the first occurrence; twice A on subsequent occurrences |
| Twice A plus `0x54` | 32 bits | P |
| B plus `0x19` | 8 bits | Low byte of A |

Within each complete repetition, the destinations are written in the table’s order. The prefix ends with another write to A plus `0x11`, followed by another write of twice A to B plus `0x34`, without the final two writes of a complete repetition.

There are no visible data reads, calls, conditional branches, or input-validation operations within this prefix. The byte values and address expressions derive from incoming values, not from reloading destination memory.

Repeated writes are part of the static behavior. They cannot generally be collapsed into one final assignment: addresses may overlap, and the memory may have externally observable write behavior. This contract does not assert a numerical count of the repeated groups.

The first write of V remains an observable operation even though later writes replace the same word destination with twice A.

### Initial captures and fixed-address updates

After the prefix, the function captures a word beginning at fixed address `0xA9`, then captures a word at P plus `0x74`.

It next writes a byte at fixed address `0x3A`. That byte is the low eight bits of twice the low byte of A, plus `0x30`. Any uncertainty about the signed interpretation of the intermediate character value does not change this final eight-bit result.

Only after this fixed-address write does the function capture:

- A word at C plus `0x14`, retained for a later restoration and for the indirect invocation.
- A word at A plus `0x04`, retained for a later word write.

The next ordered modifications establish the following intermediate state:

- C plus `0x0D` receives the low byte of A.
- A plus `0x08` receives byte `0x6C`.
- A plus `0x0D`, then A plus `0x09`, receive the low byte of B.
- C plus `0x54` receives C as a word.
- Fixed address `0x67` receives the low byte of the previously captured word from `0xA9`.

These are ordered stores, not a simultaneous record update.

### Captured-word propagation and overlapping updates

The function then reads a word at C plus `0x44`; call this captured value **R**.

The byte currently at fixed address `0x67` is copied to fixed address `0x21`. The low byte of R is written at A plus `0x10`, and R is written as a word at B plus `0x54`.

A byte derived from right-shifting R is then written at C plus `0x01`. The shift count is the low eight bits of A. The supplied representation uses a signed right shift; its behavior for negative values and counts outside the ordinary 32-bit range is not fully established.

The word at B plus `0x54` is then overwritten with A. Thus R’s earlier word store is transient but remains part of the ordered memory effects.

Next, a word is captured from A plus `0x40`. The earlier captured word from A plus `0x04` is stored at B plus `0x74`, after which the word captured from A plus `0x40` is stored at A plus `0x10`.

This word store replaces the earlier byte at A plus `0x10` and also covers offsets `0x11` through `0x13`. It can therefore replace a byte repeatedly written during the prefix. This overlap exists regardless of whether A, B, and C designate separate regions.

The subsequent ordered effects are:

- C is stored as a word at B plus `0x44`.
- The current byte at fixed address `0x67` is copied to A plus `0x09`.
- The low byte of A is written at C plus `0x09`.
- The originally captured word from fixed address `0xA9` is stored at A plus `0x44`.
- A word is read from A plus `0x54`; call this captured value **N**.
- The current byte at fixed address `0x67` is again copied to A plus `0x09`.

The two reads of fixed address `0x67` are separate memory observations. They must not be replaced by an assumption that this location still contains the value first written there: intervening stores can alias it.

### Pointer-derived updates and late captures

The low byte of the word captured from P plus `0x74` is written at N plus `0x0D`.

The earlier captured word from C plus `0x14` is then stored at C plus `0x54`, replacing the earlier store of C at that location, subject to intervening aliasing effects.

The function next captures a word from A plus `0x14`. Its low byte will later be written through N.

The remaining updates before the final pointer-derived sequence are ordered as follows:

- C plus `0x11` receives the low byte originally captured from P plus `0x74`.
- A plus `0x09` receives the low byte of N.
- A plus `0x44` receives N as a word.

The last of these replaces the earlier word written there from fixed address `0xA9`. Neither earlier value should be inferred to survive.

### Final memory sequence and indirect invocation

A word is read from C plus `0x04`; call this captured value **T**. The function writes the 16-bit value `0x0065` at the address formed by doubling T.

It then reads a word at T plus `0x64`; call this captured value **U**. This read follows the halfword write and may observe its effects if the locations overlap.

The function writes the low byte of the previously captured word from A plus `0x14` at N plus `0x11`, then writes the word value `100` at N plus `0x54`.

A word is subsequently captured from T plus `0x74`; call this value **W**. A further word is read from C plus `0x64`; call this value **L**.

Finally:

- C plus `0x09` receives the low byte of T.
- L plus `0x54` receives U as a word.
- The address formed by doubling U receives L as a word.

The unresolved indirect target is then invoked with these four arguments, in order:

1. `0x72`.
2. U.
3. The word captured earlier from C plus `0x14`.
4. W.

All four values come from constants or earlier captures. Later writes do not retroactively change those captured argument values.

### Byte truncation and arithmetic

If A’s low byte is `0xF0`, the byte written at fixed address `0x3A` is `0x10`, because twice `0xF0` plus `0x30` has low byte `0x10`.

If B’s low byte is `0xAB`, each prefix byte write at A plus `0x11` stores `0xAB`. Higher bits of B do not contribute to that byte value.

### Unsigned address wraparound

If A is `0x80000000`, twice A wraps to zero in the unsigned 32-bit expressions. The repeated P word destination is therefore address `0x54`.

This establishes arithmetic only. Successful execution additionally requires all accesses derived from that A, including accesses near `0x80000000`, to be supported.

### Capture timing under aliasing

Conversely, changing C plus `0x14` after its early capture does not change the third indirect-call argument. That argument uses the captured word, not a fresh read.
