### Widths and arithmetic

The representation uses 8-bit bytes, 16-bit stores, 32-bit words and intermediates, and some 64-bit results.

Byte reads used numerically are unsigned and zero-extended. Narrow stores retain the destination-width portion of their values.

For arithmetic examples below, the **32-bit bit-vector interpretation** means that shifts, addition, subtraction, and multiplication retain their low 32 bits. This interpretation is conditional, not a claim that all represented signed operations have established executable semantics.

Important width relationships include:

- A left shift by eight discards the original high eight bits under that interpretation.
- Masks `0xFFFFFF`, `0x3FFFFF`, `0x7FFFF`, and `0xFFFFF` retain 24, 22, 19, and 20 bits, respectively.
- A recurring masked extraction retains bits 10 through 19 before relocating them.
- Right-shifting a represented 64-bit result by more than 31 positions extracts information from its high portion, not from its low word.
- An index near the first stores is obtained by retaining the low 15 bits of an already eight-bit-left-shifted word, then shifting right by six. Its conditional range is zero through 508 in multiples of four.

Explicitly assigned zero intermediates remain zero through represented shifts until reassigned. Intervening calls do not, by themselves, turn these represented saved values into unknowns. This does not imply that callees preserve global memory or unspecified secondary results.

Signed overflow, negative signed left shifts, signed right shifts, excessive shift counts, and address wrapping remain **documented_only** where not resolved by the representation.

### Initial explicit memory mutations

The first four direct stores occur in this order:

1. A 32-bit value is stored through the final paired result’s high word plus `0x14`.
2. A byte derived from that high word is stored at a saved shifted base plus nine.
3. A saved scaled value is stored as a word through the final result’s low word multiplied by 256, plus `0x54`.
4. The byte at the saved shifted base plus nine is overwritten with the low byte of another saved value.

Both byte writes are represented accesses. Their final-state overlap does not authorize collapsing them.

The next group clears a word at a scaled base plus `0x14`, reads an unsigned byte at that base plus five, clears a word at twice the narrow index plus `0x14`, and stores the zero-extended byte at the first base plus `0x34`. It then reads back the first base’s `0x14` word, repeats the other clear, and writes the read-back word’s low byte twice at the second base plus `0x15`.

That read-back is not unconditionally zero. Intervening stores can overlap the earlier cleared word.

An absolute byte read at `0x05` precedes an absolute byte write at `0x01`. The loaded byte is then used, zero-extended, as another address base.

For describing the following relationships:

- The **working base** is four times the adjusted base.
- The **saved burst base** is the value loaded from the global word after the overwrite group described next.

These are descriptive roles, not recovered object identities.

The working base plus `0x20` receives four ordered word writes: `0x20`, then `0x2F`, then an unsigned byte widened from the working base plus four, then `0x20`. The byte read occurs after the first two writes and before the final two complete.

**After all four overwrites, the global word is loaded and retained as the saved burst base. Only then is the absolute word at `0x60` written with `0x20`.**

This load position is significant. Earlier computed writes may have changed the global storage before the load. Conversely, the absolute `0x60` write or later computed writes may overlap that storage after the load. Neither permits replacing the saved value with a later reread.

The ensuing burst links four destinations through saved scalar values:

- A byte at the saved burst base plus `0x19`, receiving the working base’s low byte.
- A byte at the working base plus `0x11`, receiving the saved burst base’s low byte.

## Acceptance examples

These are conditional static consequences, not runtime results.

1. **Initial forwarding.** With second argument three, the initial call receives three and 192 in its second and third represented argument positions. The first argument is unchanged.

2. **Width-limited shift.** Under 32-bit bit-vector arithmetic, second-argument pattern `0x04000000` yields zero after shifting left by six. Forwarding a widened nonzero result would differ.

3. **Paired-result separation.** Where a call forwards a paired result’s low word shifted by eight and its high word unchanged, low word one and high word two produce arguments 256 and two.

4. **Narrow index.** Under the stated bit-vector interpretation, the narrow index before direct stores is a multiple of four between zero and 508.

5. **Saved burst base.** If the global word supplies one address when loaded and a subsequent overlapping write changes its storage, burst accesses still use the saved address. The load must occur after the four working-base-plus-`0x20` overwrites and before the absolute `0x60` write.

6. **Interleaved final read.** If the final-phase `0x3A` byte write overlaps the subsequent offset-`0x14` word read, that read observes the post-write word under the environment’s byte-order rules. Reading the word before that byte write would not preserve the represented order.

7. **Independent `0x67` rereads.** If an intervening computed write changes `0x67` between its two later reads, the two working-base-plus-nine byte stores can receive different values. Caching the initial `0x67` assignment for both is not equivalent.

8. **Different third store source.** If the loaded working-base-plus-`0x54` word has low byte `0xB7` while `0x67` contains `0x42`, the subsequent third working-base-plus-nine store writes `0xB7`, not `0x42`.

9. **Partial failure.** If absolute address `0x01` is inaccessible, no graceful error return or reversal of preceding calls and stores is established.

10. **Final handoff.** If every preceding operation completes and the final target is callable, its first represented argument is `0x72`; its other arguments preserve the specified earlier and later read values.
