#### Selector and lookup boundaries

Rounds 1, 2, 3, and 9 pass single-bit selectors. Bits above the selected bit do not influence that selector, although dependencies receiving the full primary value can still observe them.

Round 5 uses unsigned 32-bit subtraction followed by extraction of bit 31. It is not generally an unsigned comparison against 7:

- High half 6 produces selector 1.
- High half 7 produces selector 0.
- High half `0x80000007` produces selector 1.

Round 6 instead interprets the high half as a signed 32-bit value and subtracts 7. When that subtraction is representable, its sign is extended through a right shift by 31, producing zero or negative one in the represented operation. Subtracting this mask from `0x10` yields:

- `0x10` for a nonnegative difference.
- `0x11` for a negative difference.

The signed subtraction is representable only when the signed high-half value is at least \(-0x7ffffff9\). Lower signed values overflow when 7 is subtracted. Behavior for those inputs is **documented_only**, pending instruction-level confirmation; a wrapping subtraction must not be substituted as established behavior. The represented sign-extending shift also lacks independent instruction-level verification.

The byte reads in rounds 4 and 6 occur after the intervening no-explicit-argument call. They must not be treated as values cached before that call.

## Acceptance examples

These are conditional static examples, not executed tests. Each assumes earlier operations complete and required memory is accessible unless the example specifically concerns unresolved behavior.

1. **Input bit isolation.** Input `0x01000000` gives round 1 selector 1; input `0x02000000` gives selector 0. Each full input still reaches its initial dependency unchanged.

2. **Byte-read timing.** If round 4’s primary value has bit 29 clear, its byte address is `0x10`; otherwise it is `0x11`. A dependency change to that byte before the read must remain observable.

3. **Unsigned subtraction boundary.** In round 5, high halves 6, 7, and `0x80000007` produce selectors 1, 0, and 1 respectively. An unsigned “less than 7” replacement fails the third case.

4. **Representable signed doubling.** Address factors `0x3fffffff` and `-0x40000000` double to `0x7ffffffe` and `-0x80000000`, respectively, without signed arithmetic overflow. Neither result establishes an accessible destination.

5. **Overflowing signed doubling.** Factors `0x40000000` and `-0x40000001` lie outside the representable-doubling interval. No particular wrapped address or completed store is accepted as established behavior for either case.

6. **Signed lookup boundary.** In round 6, signed high halves 6 and 7 yield differences -1 and 0, selecting `0x11` and `0x10`. A signed high half of `-0x80000000` overflows on subtraction of 7; its lookup address remains **documented_only**.

7. **Record-address wraparound.** Entry-state bits representing `0x32` yield record-read address `0xff`; bits representing `0x33` yield address zero. This calculation is unsigned, unlike the initial signed doubling.

8. **Record preservation.** Following successful reads, output words at `0xcb` and `0xcf` contain the captured input words from offsets `8` and `16`. Input words from offsets `0` and `4` instead become the first and fourth explicit arguments to **F-U04**.

9. **Status 9.** The state-base-offset-`0x68` write occurs, followed by the represented invalid-instruction boundary. **F-U05** and the conditional byte and halfword stores are not reached.

10. **Status 8.** The later path stores byte 8 at state-base offset `0x0f` and targets address `0x30` for the halfword store.

11. **Saved status.** If the sampled status is 3 and **F-U05** changes only the original global status, the later stores still use 3: byte value 3 and halfword destination `0x2b`.
